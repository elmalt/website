Job-market paper

The Doctor’s Pen and the Payer’s Bargain: Insurer Ownership of Primary Care

When a national insurer bought the largest primary care group in a local market, rival insurers came to pay about 13 percent more for the same office visit there, and Medicare paid more for the insurer’s own enrollees while their care did not rise.

Abstract

Lawmakers are considering bans on insurer ownership of physician practices. Such vertical integration can be efficient, if it eliminates double marginalization or otherwise disciplines prices; consumers gain if the savings are passed through to premiums. It can also be harmful, through foreclosure of rival insurers or inflation of government subsidies. I study five such channels in one case study: a national insurer’s 2020 acquisition of the largest primary care group in a local Oregon market, using all-payer claims from 2017 to 2023. On the side of efficiency, the integrated insurer does not obtain lower hospital prices at the dominant local hospital system, and although the acquired clinics refer its enrollees to specialists about 21 percent less often than rival insurers’ enrollees at the same clinics, with no rise in emergency department visits or hospital admissions, its Medicare Advantage premiums do not fall relative to rivals’. On the side of harm, integration leads rival insurers to pay about 13 percent more for the same commercial office visit at the acquired clinics, and Medicare pays more for enrollees of the integrated insurer after the acquisition, while their use of hospital, emergency and specialist care and their total spending do not rise. In a market with hospital and physician concentration typical of the U.S., fragmented commercial insurers and high Medicare Advantage enrollment, I find these harms, and any savings were not passed on to consumers.

Hospitals, Insurers, and the Variation in Negotiated Prices: Evidence from Transparency in Coverage Data

November 2026

Explore prices at hospitals in your market →

Abstract

The price of a hip or knee replacement in Dallas ranges from $12,000 to $64,000 across hospitals. I use the Transparency in Coverage files of UnitedHealthcare, the Blue Cross Blue Shield plans and Cigna, 416 TB of negotiated rates at 4,075 U.S. hospitals, to ask who drives this variation and whether insurers bargain harder for the plans whose costs they bear. A bias-corrected AKM decomposition attributes 49% of the variance of log prices to hospitals and 13% to insurers. Funding type does not change prices within a product. In the 208,209 hospital–service cells where UnitedHealthcare sells the same product to self-funded and fully insured employers, the two prices are the same to within 0.5%. Fully insured employers instead buy narrower networks. Of fully insured employer plan files, 37% use a narrow network, compared with 3% of self-funded files, and these fully insured narrow networks pay 8% less at the same hospital.

ESG Investing: Only Part of the Climate Puzzle

With Deniz Igan and Divya Kirti
CEPR Discussion Paper 16061 · 2021 · Revised September 2023

Abstract

In the absence of sufficient public support for carbon taxes, a more sustainable approach to finance—one that incorporates environmental, social, and governance (ESG) considerations—could be part of the way forward to address climate change. However, our analysis finds no robust link between ESG scores and carbon footprints of major upstream firms—on the supply side of emission-intensive production chains. ESG scores tend to reflect what these firms say they (will) do, not what they actually do, to contain their carbon footprints. Comprehensive disclosure requirements in this important area—and continued efforts to build consensus for effective economy-wide policies targeting carbon emissions—remain crucial.
